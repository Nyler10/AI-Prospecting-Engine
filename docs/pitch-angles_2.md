# Structuring Pitch Angles by Vertical

A single generic pitch underperforms because different verticals value different things, even when the underlying product is the same. This defines a pattern for giving an agent a distinct, defensible angle per vertical rather than one template stretched across all of them.

## Per-vertical structure

For each vertical or segment you sell into, define:

1. **The primary hook** — the one thing this vertical cares about most that your product addresses. Not a feature list — the single sharpest reason this specific buyer should care.
2. **The framing** — whether the pitch should be positioned as additive (layered onto what they already have) or as a genuine replacement. Getting this wrong is one of the most common ways a cold pitch gets ignored — an additive pitch to a buyer who's actively shopping to replace something reads as tone-deaf, and vice versa.
3. **Competitive positioning** — for the one or two competitors or alternatives most likely to already be in this vertical's consideration set, the specific differentiators to lead with (not a generic "we're better" claim).
4. **Buying-process notes** — anything about this vertical's budget cycle, typical stakeholders, or decision timeline that should change the pacing or tone of outreach, separate from the message content itself.

## The cross-vertical rule

However well-defined the vertical template is, it should never be the entire pitch. The vertical determines the *angle*; prospect-specific research (recent news, current setup, business model) supplies the *specifics* that make the outreach feel researched rather than templated. An agent that sends the vertical template verbatim, with only the company name swapped in, has failed at this step even if the vertical logic itself was correct.
